Mass Incarceration in the US

Prisons as a social institution, not only a penal one. A St. Stephen’s paper with Arz Taneja and Sakshi Singh, later in Economic and Political Weekly.

WithArz Taneja, Sakshi Singh
DataNational Longitudinal Survey of Youth, 1979–2020
LensInequality, race, schooling, family, work

The argument

Confinement concentrates inequality, then sends it back out as stigma, unemployment, poor health, and family instability. The inequality is hard to see, it accumulates, and it travels across generations. Incarceration, read this way, sits at odds with the ideals the prison claims.

Western’s point is the one the paper starts from: the American prison would matter less if it only held the people inside it. The boom of the last decades is an intervention in class and race, a predictor of life trajectories for specific backgrounds. Pager’s revolving door is the through-line: release without a path out of the credential of a record, then a return that further shrinks what a person, and the people around them, can do.

The window

NLSY is a rare panel that still follows people after they have been incarcerated. The paper reads that file from 1979 to 2020: race, schooling, gender, income, family size. The empirical picture is lopsided, highest among young African American men who have not finished high school.

Dropping the prison population from employment surveys hides how large the inequality is, and it makes worse policy.

The work is an enquiry into the American penal society, not a memo with a single lever.
